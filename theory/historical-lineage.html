Historical Lineage

This is a lineage of ideas, not a comprehensive history. It traces how democratic socialism came to define itself, and it leaves out far more than it includes. We abbreviate democratic socialism as DemSoc after this first mention. The thread that matters most runs through one split, described below, that still organizes the tradition today.

Early socialist thought

DemSoc's roots lie in the socialist thought that grew up alongside industrial capitalism in nineteenth-century Europe, as new industrial working classes and their advocates asked who should own and control the factories, and for whose benefit. Early socialists ranged from those who tried to build model cooperative communities to the more systematic theorists who followed. Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels gave the broader movement its most influential body of theory, arguing that capitalism concentrated ownership and generated class conflict and that history was tending toward collective ownership of the means of production, meaning the factories, land, and capital used to produce goods. Their work is foundational to the whole socialist family, including the branches that later rejected their political conclusions.

The foundational split

The defining division in this history is not between socialism and capitalism but within socialism itself: between those who pursued socialism through democratic and parliamentary means, and those who pursued it through revolution and, once in power, one-party rule. This split is the spine of the modern DemSoc tradition, because DemSoc defines itself by taking the first road and rejecting the second.

The division hardened in the early twentieth century. The Russian Revolution of 1917 produced the first state to call itself socialist, built on one-party control rather than competitive elections, and the international movement divided over whether to follow that example. Parties that held to elections and civil liberties became the ancestors of today's social democratic and democratic socialist currents; parties that followed the revolutionary model became the communist ones. The taxonomy explains how these two now differ.

Evolutionary and revisionist currents

Even before 1917, a current within European socialism argued that socialism could and should be reached gradually, through the vote, reform, and the growth of working-class institutions, rather than through a single revolutionary rupture. This evolutionary or revisionist position held that expanding democracy and steadily improving workers' conditions were the real path forward, and it was contested at the time by those who held to a more orthodox revolutionary reading. Across the twentieth century, parliamentary socialist and social democratic parties governed many European countries, generally building welfare states and regulating capitalism rather than abolishing private ownership wholesale. That practical record is a large part of why social democracy and democratic socialism came to overlap so heavily.

The American thread

Socialism in the United States followed its own path. In the early twentieth century, a socialist movement associated with figures such as Eugene Debs won a visible if minority following, including in electoral politics, before declining under wartime repression, internal splits, and later the pressures of the Cold War.

For much of the mid-century, American socialism was marginal. The writer and activist Michael Harrington became one of its most prominent voices and helped found the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA), which has since become the largest DemSoc organization in the country.

Observers widely describe a resurgence of American democratic socialism after 2016, pointing to growth in DSA membership and to the election of self-identified democratic socialists. We attribute this framing to those observers rather than endorsing it, and we note that its long-run significance is not yet settled.

Where the tradition sits today

Today the tradition is plural rather than unified. It runs from people whose practical program is hard to distinguish from social democracy to those who hold to the longer-run goal of social ownership, and it continues to define itself by the democratic commitment that came out of the foundational split. For who currently identifies with the label, see who identifies as a democratic socialist. For how these ideas show up in real economies, see the case studies. The internal disagreement about how far the tradition should ultimately go is taken up on coexistence with capitalism.
