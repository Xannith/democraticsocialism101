Coexistence With Capitalism

This page answers the question the whole site is built around: can democratic socialism share an economy with capitalism, and if so, how? We abbreviate democratic socialism as DemSoc after this first mention. The honest answer begins by noticing that coexistence is not one position but a range, and that people who use the DemSoc label sit at different points on it.

The spectrum, not a single position

We lay the range out without ranking it, because ranking it is itself the internal disagreement, taken up further down.

At one end is reform within capitalism: keep a mostly private, market economy and make it fairer through regulation, a strong welfare state, and stronger labor, a position that overlaps almost entirely with social democracy. In the middle are structural reforms, meaning changes that gradually shift ownership and control themselves, not only incomes, for example by growing cooperative and public sectors or giving workers and the public direct stakes. At the far end is gradual transcendence: treating those same reforms as steps toward an economy in which social ownership predominates, still reached by democratic and consensual means. The difference between the middle and the far end is often not the immediate policy but where the policy is meant to lead.

Mechanisms of coexistence

Whatever the intended destination, coexistence relies on concrete mechanisms that already exist in mixed economies. Each is a way for social or democratic control to operate inside a market system.

  • Codetermination: giving workers a formal voice in company decisions, such as seats on boards, which shares control without transferring ownership. Germany is the leading example (see the Germany case study and the glossary entry).
  • Sectoral decommodification: taking some sectors, such as healthcare, largely out of the market and providing them publicly, while most of the economy stays private. Decommodification means removing something from being bought and sold as an ordinary commodity. The Nordic and British health systems are examples (see the Nordic model and United Kingdom case studies).
  • Public options: a publicly run provider that competes alongside private ones, such as a public bank or insurer, letting people choose it without banning the private alternative. The Bank of North Dakota is a real instance (see the United States case study).
  • Sovereign and social wealth funds: publicly owned investment funds that hold assets on behalf of the public. A sovereign wealth fund is typically built from state revenues such as oil; a social wealth fund is a proposal to give the public collective ownership stakes and share the returns. Norway's fund is the best-known existing example (see the Nordic model case study).
  • Cooperative sectors: worker-owned firms operating within a market economy, which place social ownership at the level of the firm. Mondragon is the largest example (see the Mondragon case study and the glossary entry).

None of these, on its own, ends capitalism. Each is a way democratic control coexists with it.

The internal debate: destination or waypoint

This is where the tradition genuinely divides, and we present both sides at full strength.

Reform as destination. On this view, a well-regulated mixed economy with strong public services, worker voice, and a healthy cooperative sector is not a stepping stone to something else; it is the goal. Proponents argue it delivers most of what people actually want from socialism, meaning security, fairness, and democratic voice, while keeping the innovation and pluralism of markets and avoiding the concentration of power that undid one-party states. To them, promising to transcend capitalism is both unnecessary and a political liability.

Reform as waypoint. On this view, reforms that leave the basic ownership structure intact are always vulnerable, because concentrated private wealth keeps the power to erode them, so lasting change requires gradually shifting ownership itself. Proponents argue the aim should be structural reforms that build public and worker power over time, moving toward an economy where social ownership predominates, still democratically. To them, treating the mixed economy as the destination gives up the point.

Neither position is the settled view of the tradition. Where a given democratic socialist stands on this question is often the most substantive thing that distinguishes them from another.

The critics, from both directions

Coexistence draws fire from both sides, and the two criticisms are close to mirror images. The symmetry is deliberate, because it is real.

From the free-market direction, critics argue the hybrid is unstable toward too much state. On this view, each expansion of public provision, regulation, and taxation dampens investment and growth, and the cost of an ever-larger public and welfare sector eventually forces either retrenchment or decline; the mixed economy, they argue, tends to drift toward stagnation unless checked.

From the socialist direction, critics argue the hybrid is unstable toward too little. On this view, reforms that stop short of changing ownership are routinely rolled back or captured, because the owners of capital keep the economic and political leverage to undo them, so coexistence tends to drift back toward ordinary capitalism rather than beyond it.

One camp says the mix cannot hold because it does too much; the other says it cannot hold because it does too little. Both cannot be fully right, and the disagreement is as much empirical as theoretical. We take these objections up again, alongside the others, on the critiques and responses page.
