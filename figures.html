Who Identifies as a Democratic Socialist

This page lists only public figures who publicly self-identify as democratic socialists, cited to their own statements or to documented membership in the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) or an equivalent party. We do not include anyone on the basis of a label applied by others, whether by supporters or by opponents. We abbreviate democratic socialism as DemSoc after this first mention. Self-identification and affiliation can change, so every entry states the documentary basis it rests on, and we expand the list only when we can cite a person's own words or a documented membership record.

Last verified: 2026-07-23

How this list is organized

The single rule has not changed: a person's own words, or a membership that itself constitutes the claim, and never a label applied by someone else. What has changed is that we now sort entries by which kind of evidence supports them, because those kinds are not equally strong and collapsing them into one list would hide that.

  • Tier 1, self-identified. The person used the words in public or in a documented private statement. Each entry quotes the statement, names the source, and gives the date.
  • Tier 2, documented members. The person held membership in the DSA, the Socialist Party of America, or an equivalent democratic socialist party abroad. Membership is the claim here, not a quote.
  • Tier 3, historical canon. Figures the tradition consistently claims as its own. Where the DemSoc label is an inference drawn by others rather than a self-description, we say so in the entry and quote the actual documentary basis instead.
  • Annex, policy without the identity. Figures who advanced adjacent policy and never identified with the tradition. They are listed because readers encounter the claim constantly, not because we accept it.

Entries frequently qualify for more than one tier, and we note that where it happens. Where the evidence is contested, secondhand, or still being confirmed, the entry says so plainly rather than reading as though it were settled. Every living United States elected official carries a dated status line, because endorsements and memberships shift without public announcement and an undated status label is worth very little.

Tier 1: Self-identified in their own words

These are the cleanest inclusions on the page. Each rests on a statement the person made themselves, quoted verbatim.

George Orwell

Writer, died 1950. "Every line of serious work that I have written since 1936 has been written, directly or indirectly, against totalitarianism and for democratic socialism, as I understand it." Source: "Why I Write," 1946. This is the strongest historical self-identification on the roster, both because it uses the exact phrase and because Orwell wrote it as a statement of his own settled purpose.

Olof Palme

Prime Minister of Sweden, assassinated 1986. Palme publicly explained why he was a democratic socialist in a party-leadership debate against Falldin and Ullsten during the 1982 election, preserved in SVT footage. In a 1972 speech held at the Arbetarrorelsens arkiv he stated, "we are democratic socialists, because we are convinced of the ability of reformism to accomplish things ... worthwhile for humanity."

Bernie Sanders

United States Senator from Vermont. Sanders has used the democratic socialist label for decades. At Georgetown University on November 19, 2015 he said, "When I use the word socialist - and I know some people are uncomfortable about it - I say that it is imperative that we create a political revolution." He swore in Zohran Mamdani as mayor of New York City on January 1, 2026. Caveat: Sanders is an independent and not a formal DSA member, so he appears in Tier 1 on the strength of his own words alone. His policy program is often classified by analysts as closer to social democracy, a point discussed on the taxonomy page.

Status verified: 2026-07-23

Zohran Mamdani

112th Mayor of New York City, sworn in January 1, 2026. Per the official transcript of his inauguration address released by the New York City Mayor's Office on January 1, 2026: "We will govern without shame and insecurity, making no apology for what we believe. I was elected as a democratic socialist and I will govern as a democratic socialist. I will not abandon my principles for fear of being deemed radical." He had earlier told the New York Post in 2025, "I'm proud to be a democratic socialist." Mamdani is also a DSA member, so he qualifies under both Tier 1 and Tier 2, and he is the highest-profile serving DSA member in the United States.

Status verified: 2026-07-23

Ron Dellums

United States Representative from California, later Mayor of Oakland, died 2018. In the debut issue of In These Times in November 1976 he said, "I think democratic socialism will ultimately prevail in this country because it makes an enormous amount of sense." He described himself as a Socialist and was the first self-described socialist in Congress since Victor Berger. Dellums also qualifies under Tier 2, having served as a DSA vice chair during the 1980s.

Martin Luther King Jr.

Civil rights leader, assassinated 1968. The documentary basis is three statements, none of them a public adoption of the label as a personal identity. In a letter to Coretta Scott dated July 18, 1952 he wrote, "I imagine you already know that I am much more socialistic in my economic theory than capitalistic ... So today capitalism has outlived its usefulness." In a 1961 speech to the Negro American Labor Council he said, "Call it democracy, or call it democratic socialism, but there must be a better distribution of wealth within this country." Addressing his own staff in 1966 he said "there must be better distribution of wealth and maybe America must move toward a democratic socialism."

Annotation: King used the phrase privately and descriptively, and he did not publicly adopt it as a personal identity, a choice generally read as strategic. This entry is a Tier 1 and Tier 3 hybrid. The evidence is the 1952 letter and the internal addresses, not any public self-labeling, and we state that here rather than presenting him as a clean Tier 1 case.

Jacinda Ardern

Former Prime Minister of New Zealand. Branko Marcetic, writing in Jacobin in January 2023, described "Ardern, who has described herself as a 'democratic socialist'." As incoming Prime Minister in 2017 she called capitalism a "blatant failure" given the country's child poverty levels.

Annotation: Ardern governed as, and more often described herself as, a social democrat. The self-label is contested and shifted over her career, and the sourcing above is secondary rather than a primary transcript. Included with that caveat.

Jawaharlal Nehru

Prime Minister of India, died 1964. In his Congress presidential address at Lahore in December 1929 he said, "I must frankly confess that I am a socialist and a republican." At Avadi in 1954 he said, "The picture I have in mind is definitely and absolutely a socialistic picture of society."

Annotation: Nehru was Fabian-influenced and is frequently classified as a social democrat. The "socialistic pattern of society" he pursued was a mixed economy rather than social ownership of major industry. He said "socialist," not "democratic socialist." Included with that caveat.

Salvador Allende

President of Chile and a member of the Socialist Party of Chile, died 1973. Allende built his political career on what he called the democratic road to socialism, and he was the first Marxist elected head of state in open balloting. He qualifies under Tier 2 as well, on party membership.

Annotation: Allende is better described as a pragmatic Marxist than as a reformist democratic socialist in the DSA sense. He belongs on this page because of the democratic route he insisted on, not because his program matched the tradition this site describes. Included with heavy annotation.

Erlander, Bevan, and Attlee

Three further figures with strong self-identification and party records, grouped here because the basis in each case is similar. Tage Erlander, Prime Minister of Sweden, described himself as a socialist and remained committed to expanding social ownership. Aneurin Bevan, the Labour minister who was the architect of the National Health Service, delivered the 1950 Fabian lecture on socialist values. Clement Attlee, Labour Prime Minister, was an Independent Labour Party socialist from his years in Stepney onward.

Tier 2: Documented members

Entries here rest on membership in the DSA, the Socialist Party of America, or an equivalent democratic socialist party abroad. In this tier the membership record is the claim. We do not require a separate quote, and where we have one we note it.

Historical anchors

Eugene V. Debs

Five-time presidential candidate of the Socialist Party of America. Debs is the central figure of the American democratic socialist tradition and appears in both Tier 2 and Tier 3.

Norman Thomas

Six-time presidential candidate of the Socialist Party of America, and Debs's successor as the party's national figure. Tier 2 and Tier 3.

Michael Harrington

Founder and most influential early leader of the DSA, which formed in March 1982 from the merger of the Democratic Socialist Organizing Committee and the New American Movement. Author of "The Other America," published in 1962, the book widely credited with putting American poverty back on the national agenda.

Victor Berger and Meyer London

Victor Berger, Socialist Party United States Representative from Wisconsin, was the first Socialist to serve in Congress. Meyer London, Socialist Party United States Representative from New York, was the second.

The Milwaukee sewer socialists

Emil Seidel, Daniel Hoan, and Frank Zeidler served as Socialist Party mayors of Milwaukee, governing on a program of clean water, public sanitation, parks, and honest administration that gave the movement its nickname. Zeidler, who served from 1948 to 1960, was the last Socialist mayor of a major American city.

Cornel West

Philosopher and honorary chair of the DSA, documented on the organization's own site.

Annotation: West remains associated with the tradition but ran an independent presidential campaign in 2024 rather than working through the DSA's electoral strategy. Readers should treat the honorary chairmanship and his current political practice as two separate facts.

Frances Fox Piven

Political scientist and long-time DSA member who served on the organization's board and its national executive committee in 1985.

Barbara Ehrenreich

Writer and DSA co-chair alongside Michael Harrington from 1983. Died 2022.

Gloria Steinem

Documented DSA participant during the 1980s, appearing on the New Directions and American Solidarity rosters. Listed here as a historical member. We have not verified any current affiliation and do not assert one.

David Dinkins and Major Owens

David Dinkins, Mayor of New York City, was a documented DSA-affiliated elected official in the 1980s. Major Owens, United States Representative from New York, was a DSA member.

A. Philip Randolph and Bayard Rustin

Both were Socialist Party members and democratic socialists, and both organized at the point where the labor movement and the civil rights movement met. They appear in Tier 3 as canon as well.

Current and recent United States electeds

Every entry in this subsection carries a dated status line. DSA endorsements and memberships change, sometimes without any public announcement, so a status label here is only as good as the date attached to it.

Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez

United States Representative from New York and a DSA member. She won her 2018 congressional primary as a DSA-endorsed candidate. On July 10, 2024 the DSA's National Political Committee voted to withdraw its conditional national endorsement, announcing the following day that "the NPC is withdrawing our conditional endorsement of Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, although she will remain endorsed by our New York City chapter." The stated trigger was her position on Israel and Palestine. The national withdrawal and the continuing New York City chapter endorsement are two separate facts, and we report them separately rather than summarizing them as a single relationship.

Rashida Tlaib

United States Representative from Michigan, reported as an incumbent DSA member as of mid-2025.

Greg Casar

United States Representative from Texas, reported as an incumbent DSA member as of mid-2025.

Status verified: 2026-07-23

Summer Lee

United States Representative from Pennsylvania, DSA-affiliated. We list her as affiliated rather than as a confirmed member, which is the stronger claim and one we have not verified.

Status verified: 2026-07-23

Cori Bush

Former United States Representative from Missouri, DSA-endorsed. She lost her 2024 primary and no longer holds office.

Status verified: 2026-07-23

Jamaal Bowman

Former United States Representative from New York and a DSA member. His relationship with the organization has a documented history worth stating in full. The DSA's National Political Committee declined to expel him in a statement dated December 2, 2021, but withheld its 2022 re-endorsement "unless he is able to demonstrate solidarity with Palestine in alignment with expectations we have set." The stated triggers were his September 25, 2021 vote for roughly 1.1 billion dollars in Iron Dome funding and a November 2021 J Street trip during which he met Israeli Prime Minister Naftali Bennett. He was later re-endorsed by New York City DSA. He lost his 2024 primary and no longer holds office.

New York City DSA state and local officials

Julia Salazar, Jabari Brisport, Phara Souffrant Forrest, Marcela Mitaynes, and Emily Gallagher hold state and local office as New York City DSA members.

Status verified: 2026-07-23

Claire Valdez

New York Assembly member and a longtime New York City DSA leader, described by the chapter as a "proud democratic socialist." She is a 2026 congressional candidate.

Status verified: 2026-07-23

Chicago DSA aldermen

Carlos Ramirez-Rosa, Rossana Rodriguez-Sanchez, Byron Sigcho-Lopez, Daniel La Spata, and Jeanette Taylor serve on the Chicago City Council as Chicago DSA members.

Status verified: 2026-07-23

Andre Vasquez

Alderman for Chicago's 40th ward. What is documented: Chicago DSA censured him on November 24, 2020 as the only Chicago DSA council member to support Mayor Lightfoot's 2021 budget and its property tax increase, the chapter stating that he "sided with an austerity regime and the Democratic Party establishment over the interests of Chicago's working class." Chicago Magazine reported in January 2023 that he "still calls himself a democratic socialist" but did not seek the chapter's endorsement.

Status under review: The frequently repeated claim that Vasquez formally left the DSA is not cleanly documented, and we do not assert it. His current membership status is unresolved on the evidence we hold, and this entry will be corrected once a primary source settles it.

Status verified: 2026-07-23

Franklin Bynum (Houston judge), Lee Carter (Virginia delegate, since defeated), Mik Pappas (Pennsylvania magisterial district judge), India Walton (2021 Buffalo mayoral nominee), Dean Preston and Jackie Fielder (San Francisco), Nithya Raman, Hugo Soto-Martinez, and Eunisses Hernandez (Los Angeles City Council), Konstantine Anthony (Burbank), and Elizabeth Fiedler, Rick Krajewski, and Nikil Saval (Pennsylvania, Saval in the state senate).

Brandon Johnson

Mayor of Chicago, elected with DSA backing. We list him on the basis of that backing only. His personal membership status is not confirmed here and we do not assert it.

Sara Innamorato

Allegheny County Executive in Pennsylvania.

International party members

The United Kingdom Labour left

Keir Hardie founded the Independent Labour Party in 1893. Tony Benn, Michael Foot, Barbara Castle, and Jeremy Corbyn each led or represented the party's socialist left across the twentieth and twenty-first centuries. Anthony Crosland, author of "The Future of Socialism," belongs here too, with the annotation that he was a revisionist who deliberately tempered the party's commitment to public ownership and is read by many as having moved Labour toward social democracy.

Canada: the CCF and NDP tradition

J.S. Woodsworth founded the Co-operative Commonwealth Federation. Tommy Douglas, the CCF and NDP premier of Saskatchewan, is known as the father of Canadian medicare. Ed Broadbent, Jack Layton, and Jagmeet Singh led the New Democratic Party in later decades.

Annotation: Broadbent, Layton, and Singh are each better described as increasingly social democratic than as democratic socialists in the founders' sense. Broadbent himself modeled the NDP on Swedish social democracy and defended what he called the party's social democratic roots.

Continental Europe

Bruno Kreisky, Chancellor of Austria, of the SPO. Leon Blum, Prime Minister of France, of the SFIO. Jean Jaures, the French socialist leader assassinated in 1914.

Annotation: Blum and Jaures should be read within the French socialist tradition, which divides the ground between socialism and social democracy differently than the Anglo-American usage this site otherwise follows.

Gabriel Boric

President of Chile, elected at the head of a broad left coalition that includes democratic socialist parties.

Willy Brandt

Chancellor of West Germany and leader of the SPD.

Annotation: Brandt is properly classified as a social democrat, and he is included here only with that caveat, because he is so frequently cited in democratic socialist company that omitting him would leave the question unanswered. See the Germany case study for the SPD's own movement on this question.

Tier 3: Historical canon

These are figures the tradition consistently claims. Some held party cards, some did not. Where the DemSoc label is an inference drawn by later writers rather than something the person said about themselves, the entry states that and quotes what they actually wrote instead. That distinction is the whole point of this tier.

Albert Einstein

Physicist. In "Why Socialism?", published in Monthly Review in 1949, he wrote: "I am convinced there is only one way to eliminate these grave evils, namely through the establishment of a socialist economy."

Annotation: Einstein argued for a socialist economy with democratic safeguards. He warned in the same essay that "a planned economy is not yet socialism," and he stressed the protection of individual rights against the concentrated power such an economy would create. He did not use the term "democratic socialist." Classifying him under that exact label is an inference drawn by others, which is precisely the kind of claim this site's standard excludes, so we list him on the strength of his own words and name the inference as an inference.

Helen Keller

Writer and activist, a Socialist Party member and a supporter of the Industrial Workers of the World. Her socialism is the least discussed part of a very widely told life story.

Upton Sinclair and Jack London

Upton Sinclair, novelist and Socialist Party member, later led the End Poverty in California movement and ran for governor on it. Jack London, novelist, was a Socialist Party member.

Kate Richards O'Hare

Socialist Party organizer, orator, and one of the most effective campaigners the party had in its pre-war peak.

W.E.B. Du Bois

Scholar and civil rights leader, a socialist for most of his life.

Annotation: Du Bois's politics shifted substantially over a very long career, and he joined the Communist Party in 1961. Claiming him for the democratic socialist tradition without noting that shift would misrepresent the record.

Eduard Bernstein

German SPD theorist and the founder of evolutionary socialism, also called revisionism. His argument that socialism should be reached through democratic reform rather than revolution is core democratic socialist canon, and much of the tradition's later self-understanding descends from it.

Karl Kautsky

German SPD theorist.

Annotation: Kautsky was an orthodox Marxist rather than a reformist, and he opposed Bernstein's revisionism. He belongs in this tier because of his later and bitter break with the Bolsheviks over democracy, which made him an important source for the tradition's insistence that socialism without democracy is not socialism.

Bertrand Russell

Philosopher, a Fabian-influenced democratic socialist and an early critic of Soviet practice from the left.

The Fabians

H.G. Wells, George Bernard Shaw, Sidney Webb, and Beatrice Webb built the Fabian Society and with it the case for reaching socialism gradually, through existing democratic institutions.

Annotation: Sidney and Beatrice Webb wrote a late apologia for the Soviet Union that badly misjudged what they were describing. It is part of the record and the tradition has had to reckon with it.

Oscar Wilde

Writer. "The Soul of Man Under Socialism," published in 1891, argued for socialism on the grounds of individual freedom rather than material equality, which is why the tradition keeps returning to it.

Kurt Vonnegut

Novelist and a self-described socialist who repeatedly praised Eugene Debs.

Annotation: Vonnegut described himself as a socialist, not specifically as a democratic socialist. We list him on the basis of what he actually said.

Howard Zinn

Historian and a democratic socialist, best known for "A People's History of the United States."

John Dewey and Erich Fromm

John Dewey, philosopher and president of the League for Industrial Democracy, is defensible in this tier as a democratic socialist by affiliation and by his written arguments for extending democracy into economic life. Erich Fromm, psychoanalyst and social theorist, was a democratic socialist humanist. Both are listed with the annotation that their inclusion rests on affiliation and writings rather than on a single self-identifying quote.

Contemporary writers and organizers

Bhaskar Sunkara founded Jacobin and is a DSA member. Naomi Klein, writer and organizer. Astra Taylor, DSA member and a co-founder of the Debt Collective.

Annotation: Ursula K. Le Guin is frequently added to lists like this one. Her sympathies were anarchist, and she is better described as an anarchist or utopian socialist writer, so we do not list her as a democratic socialist.

Annex: policy without the identity

Read this framing before the entries. None of the figures below identified as a socialist of any kind, and this page does not claim they did. Several explicitly rejected the label. They appear here because their policies are constantly cited in arguments about democratic socialism, by supporters claiming them and by opponents warning about them, and because the honest response to both is to state what these figures actually did and what they actually said about the word. Policy adjacency is not identity, and this annex exists to keep the two apart.

Franklin D. Roosevelt

Policies: the New Deal, including Social Security, federal unemployment insurance, the Wagner Act protections for organized labor, and large-scale public employment programs.

On the label: in Fireside Chat number 5, delivered June 28, 1934, Roosevelt said: "A few timid people, who fear progress, will try to give you new and strange names for what we are doing. Sometimes they will call it 'Fascism', sometimes 'Communism', sometimes 'Regimentation', sometimes 'Socialism'." He cast socialism as a critics' mislabel for practical policy and never self-identified. Sources: the Fireside Chat text via the Social Security Administration history archive and the Miller Center at the University of Virginia.

Harry S. Truman

Policies: a Special Message to Congress on November 19, 1945 proposing national health insurance funded through compulsory social insurance, the first serious presidential attempt at universal coverage in the United States.

On the label: in that same message he wrote, "I repeat - what I am recommending is not socialized medicine. Socialized medicine means that all doctors work as employees of government. The American people want no such system. No such system is here proposed." Speaking at Syracuse, New York on October 10, 1952 he added, "Socialism is a scare word they have hurled at every advance the people have made in the last 20 years. Socialism is what they called public power. Socialism is what they called social security ... Socialism is their name for almost anything that helps all the people." Truman rejected the label directly while defending the policy. Sources: Truman Library Public Papers 192 and 289.

Theodore Roosevelt

Policies: the New Nationalism speech at Osawatomie, Kansas on August 31, 1910 became the foundation of the 1912 Progressive Party platform, which called for social insurance, a national health service, workmen's compensation, and a minimum wage for women.

On the label: in the speech itself he noted that a Kansas paper "frantically repudiates the statement that I am a Socialist on the ground that that is an unwarranted slander of the Socialists," a line that positions him against socialism rather than within it, and does so in his own words. Source: "The New Nationalism," 1910, via Teaching American History.

Lyndon B. Johnson

Policies: the Great Society, and Medicare and Medicaid, signed July 30, 1965 at the Truman Library in Independence, Missouri with Truman beside him. At the signing Johnson said, "No longer will older Americans be denied the healing miracle of modern medicine."

On the label: we have found no documented statement in which Johnson either rejected or embraced the socialist label for his own programs. The socialized medicine attacks came from his opponents, not from him. This entry is policy adjacency only, and we state the absence of evidence rather than filling it in. Source: The American Presidency Project, University of California Santa Barbara.

Richard Nixon

Policies: the Family Assistance Plan, a guaranteed minimum income proposed in August 1969 under the name workfare, reintroduced in 1971 and defeated in the Senate in 1972. The Environmental Protection Agency, created by Reorganization Plan Number 3 of 1970 and operational on December 2, 1970. The Comprehensive Health Insurance Plan with an employer mandate, sent to Congress on February 6, 1974, which promised that "There would be no exclusions of coverage based on the nature of the illness."

On the label: Nixon was a Republican who never identified as a socialist and campaigned against socialism throughout his career. DSA founder Michael Harrington is reported to have called the guaranteed income proposal the most radical idea since the New Deal, which is a judgment by a democratic socialist about a policy, not a claim about Nixon. Sources: The American Presidency Project, University of California Santa Barbara, and KFF Health News.

Dwight D. Eisenhower

Policies: he preserved and modestly expanded New Deal programs, including a significant Social Security expansion, and built the Interstate Highway System.

On the label: Eisenhower is the thinnest case in this annex and we include him only to answer the argument rather than to endorse it. There is no democratic socialist content in his program and no self-identification of any kind. Accepting the entitlement state as settled is not the same as advancing socialism, and this entry should be read as marking the boundary of the annex rather than as another example within it.

Contested and excluded

Lists like this one tend to grow by accumulation, because a name that appears on enough other lists starts to look verified. The entries below are figures commonly cited as democratic socialists who do not meet this page's standard. We name them and give the reason, both because the reasons are genuinely interesting and because a list is only as credible as its willingness to say who is not on it. None of this is a judgment of the people involved. It is a judgment about what the evidence supports.

Adjacent traditions, not this one

Emma Goldman was an anarchist and explicitly anti-state, which places her in direct opposition to a tradition that seeks to use democratic state power. Noam Chomsky describes himself as a libertarian socialist and an anarcho-syndicalist, and using his own words as the standard means not applying a different label to him. Dorothy Day was a Catholic Worker anarchist and pacifist. Ursula K. Le Guin is better described as an anarchist or utopian socialist. These are neighboring traditions with real disagreements with democratic socialism, and flattening them into it would misrepresent all of them.

A different socialist party

Kshama Sawant, formerly of the Seattle City Council, is a Socialist Alternative figure, which is a Trotskyist organization rather than a democratic socialist one. She joined the DSA in February 2021 while remaining a member of Socialist Alternative, launched Workers Strike Back in 2023, and per subsequent reporting left Socialist Alternative in 2024 to form a new group called Revolutionary Workers. She is running as an independent in Washington's 9th congressional district in 2026. Her DSA membership does not make her a democratic socialist in the sense this page uses, and she is excluded on that basis rather than listed with a contested label.

Social democrats rather than democratic socialists

Pedro Sanchez of the Spanish PSOE is a social democrat. Francois Mitterrand of the French Parti Socialiste is better described as a social democrat and a pragmatic socialist, particularly after his government's 1983 turn. Jean-Luc Melenchon is a left populist. Yanis Varoufakis describes himself as an erratic Marxist and a libertarian Marxist, which is his own words and not the label others apply to him. The distinction between social democracy and democratic socialism is set out on the taxonomy page, and it is the reason these names are here rather than above.

Contested national figures

Nelson Mandela led the ANC in close alliance with the South African Communist Party, and his position needs far more care than a list entry allows. Julius Nyerere built Ujamaa, a distinct African socialist tradition that deserves to be described on its own terms. David Ben-Gurion and Golda Meir were Labor Zionists of Mapai, adjacent labor socialists whose inclusion is disputed within the tradition itself. Reinhold Niebuhr was an early Socialist Party member who became a Cold War anti-socialist liberal, so any listing would have to pick which half of his career to represent.

Insufficient primary evidence

Danny DeVito, John Cusack, Susan Sarandon, Ed Asner, and Hasan Piker are frequently listed as democratic socialists. Several are DSA-sympathetic or were Sanders supporters, but on the evidence we hold none has a verbatim self-identification or a documented membership record, and sympathy is not the standard. Harvey Milk is contested, with no clean self-identification located. Dolores Huerta is often described as a DSA honorary chair; we could not verify that claim against primary DSA documentation, so we omit it rather than repeat it. Each of these would move into the list above the moment a qualifying primary source turns up.

A note on this list

Officeholders, memberships, and affiliations change over time. We review this page periodically and update the Last verified date above when we do, and every living United States elected official carries an individual status date because national and local endorsements can diverge and then diverge again. Additions are made only after we can cite a figure's own statement or documented membership, never on the basis of labels applied by others.

Some specific things would change a listing. A figure who publicly repudiates the label moves to the annex or to the excluded section. A figure who leaves a party moves to an annotated former member status rather than being deleted. A newly surfaced primary source can promote a figure from the annex or from Tier 3 into Tier 1, and that has happened before. Where an entry above says a claim is under review or unverified, that is a live note about our evidence and not a hedge, and it will be resolved in one direction or the other.

Several international entries here rest partly on secondary sourcing, including party histories and journalism, and the social democrat versus democratic socialist distinction is genuinely contested for many Nordic, German, and French figures. We would rather show you that seam than paper over it. For the definitions that seam turns on, see the taxonomy page. For the policies these figures advocate, see the United States case study.
