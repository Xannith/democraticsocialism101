What Is Democratic Socialism?

Democratic socialism is a political tradition that combines democratic government with the goal of extending democratic control into the economy. In plain terms, it starts from a familiar idea, that people should have a say in the decisions that govern their lives through free elections and civil liberties, and asks whether that same principle should reach further into economic life: workplaces, major industries, and the provision of essential services. We abbreviate democratic socialism as DemSoc after this first mention.

DemSoc is best understood as a tradition with internal debates rather than a single fixed program. People who use the label agree on some core commitments and disagree, sometimes sharply, about how far economic change should go and how fast. This page describes the commitments most of the tradition shares, then the questions it argues about internally. For precise definitions of DemSoc and neighboring systems such as social democracy and communism, see the taxonomy.

Core commitments

Political democracy and civil liberties. Across the tradition, these are treated as non-negotiable. Free and competitive elections, freedom of speech and the press, an independent judiciary, and the right to organize are not seen as things to be traded away for economic goals. Proponents argue that this is exactly the feature that separates DemSoc from the one-party states of the twentieth century. Critics question whether its economic aims can be reached without concentrating power, and the tradition's own answer is that any program requiring the suspension of democracy would, by its own definition, no longer be democratic socialism.

Economic democracy. This is the distinctive commitment, and it covers a real range of positions. At one end are proposals to expand worker cooperatives, employee ownership, and workplace voice within a largely market economy. At the other end are proposals for public or social ownership of major industries and finance. What these positions share is the claim that democratic accountability should extend to significant economic decisions, not only to government. Where a given person or group falls on this range is one of the main things that distinguishes strands of DemSoc from one another.

What democratic socialism is not

The tradition draws a sharp line between itself and authoritarian state socialism, meaning the one-party states, such as the former Soviet Union, that governed without competitive elections or civil liberties. Democratic socialists generally argue that those states failed the democratic test DemSoc treats as essential, and that concentrated, unaccountable power was a cause of their abuses rather than an incidental feature.

We present this as the tradition's own account of the distinction, not as a settled verdict. Critics respond in different ways: some accept that DemSoc is genuinely distinct from authoritarian socialism and aim their objections at its economic proposals instead; others argue that extensive social ownership tends to concentrate power regardless of intentions. We take those criticisms seriously and address them directly on the critiques and responses page. The point here is narrower: the distinction between DemSoc and authoritarian state socialism is one the tradition insists on, and understanding DemSoc requires understanding why.

How it relates to capitalism

Within DemSoc there is a genuine, unresolved disagreement about the ultimate relationship to capitalism, and it is more honest to present it as a range than as a single answer.

At one end is a reform-and-coexist position, which overlaps heavily with social democracy: keep a largely market economy, but regulate it strongly, build a robust welfare state, strengthen labor, and expand public and cooperative ownership at the margins. On this view, the aim is a fairer balance within a mixed economy rather than the end of markets. At the other end is a transcend position, which treats those same reforms as steps toward gradually moving beyond capitalism to an economy defined by social ownership, though still, in the DemSoc framing, through democratic and consensual means.

Many people who identify as democratic socialists hold positions somewhere between these poles, and some are simply more focused on immediate policy than on the long-run destination. This internal range is a frequent source of confusion, because two people can share the label and still disagree about where it ultimately leads. It is also why, in the United States in particular, self-described democratic socialists often advocate policies that analysts would classify as social democracy. We discuss that usage point on the taxonomy page.
